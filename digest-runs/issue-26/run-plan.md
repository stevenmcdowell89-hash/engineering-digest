## 1. Central question, tension and through-line

### 1.1 The central question

**Agents are in the lifecycle. Who owns them, what enforces the rules, and what evidence does the work leave behind?**

### 1.2 The central tension (one paragraph — this is the issue's spine)

Every limb of an agent operating model now has a product available for it. Identity vendors sell agent identity; hyperscalers and enterprise vendors sell control planes; platform teams ship execution sandboxes; toolmakers ship centrally administered permissions; spec toolkits ship compliance checklists; review agents ship with tunable precision. What almost none of it has is an **owner**. The decision rights — who may approve an agent, who sets the precision bar a review gate must clear before it is allowed to speak to a developer, who holds the token budget, who signs off a change an agent produced — are being resolved late, informally, or not at all. The consequence shows up as a measurement: organisations that improve one stage of the lifecycle and leave the rest unchanged get a fraction of the return, and organisations whose controls are weaker for agents than for the people they act on behalf of cannot produce the evidence their own regulated customers already ask for. The control surfaces are arriving faster than the ownership that makes them enforceable.

### S1 — Why the operating model is the variable
**Layout: `.by-the-numbers` opening panel, then prose, then one SVG.**
Opens with the gap: AI is in most of the work and almost none of it is fully delegable, which makes the residual — oversight, direction, verification, accountability — the thing being organised rather than the thing being automated. Forrester supplies the measured consequence of getting that wrong: improving the build stage in isolation returns a fraction of what the coding numbers promise, because the bottlenecks move to stages nobody re-designed. The arXiv survey supplies the frame that connects the two, naming reviewer attention as the scarce input being allocated, and supplies the capability curve that explains why every governance question arrived at the same time.
**Spectrum tags:** Anthropic = vendor telemetry and survey combined, spanning assistant through agent teams, and does not separate them cleanly. Forrester = analyst observation across orchestrated SDLC agents, band not separated. arXiv survey = consolidates other people's figures; every number in it is second-hand and traced in §7.
**Altitude guard:** no model names, no benchmark tables, no tool comparisons. The SWE-bench line is one sentence with a gloss, used only to date the arrival of the problem.

### S2 — Who owns what: roles, decision rights and the AI bill
**Layout: standalone with a prominent inline SVG (LeadDev's role-strain bars), plus a `.process-flow` for the four modes of oversight work.**
Three ownership questions, in order. **Role shape:** McKinsey's inventory of the job titles an agent operating model creates and elevates is the clearest published answer to "what does the new org chart say", and LeadDev's 600-leader survey is the measurement of what the existing role has already become — more hands-on, longer hours, a third of managers weighing a return to the IC track. **Decision rights:** the oversight paper is the section's structural finding — overseeing agents is at least four distinct activities (constraint-setting before the run, co-planning, real-time monitoring, post-hoc review), and delivery models that budget "review" as one task are systematically short. Pragmatic Engineer's survey supplies the qualitative half: the engineer/manager division is dissolving with nothing named to replace it, and accountability for AI-generated code in production is the decision right most organisations have not assigned. **The bill:** the FinOps Foundation's practitioner guide records token cost in SaaS-model AI as practitioners' top-ranked challenge, and the emerging ownership pattern — a small function between finance and engineering owning the gateway, the routing policy and the dashboards — is reported practice rather than data, and is framed as such.
**Spectrum tags:** McKinsey, LeadDev and the FinOps material **do not separate the bands** — say so. The oversight paper studies developers supervising software agents in agent-mode.
**Discipline:** McKinsey's role names cannot be attributed to McKinsey in print unless §7 clears the fetch. Pragmatic Engineer is used **qualitatively only** — no percentages beyond the sample size, and only if §7 clears the paywall check. The *State of FinOps 2026* figures (1,192 organisations, $83bn, 98% tracking, 73% over budget) are **not in the fetched FinOps document** and print only if Phase 3 pins that separate report.

### S3 — The control layer: identity, the execution boundary, and policy at runtime
**Layout: article with sidebar. Sidebar (`.sidebar-box`) = CNCF's agent-as-persona control set — identity, an accountable human owner, an expiry date, RBAC, resource quotas, policy — presented as the platform function's existing vocabulary applied to a new persona. Two SVGs.**
The section that answers what the control actually attaches to, in four moves. **Identity:** Okta's position is that the enforcement point is the agent's own identity with its own lifecycle and permissions, and its research is the pool's clearest statement of the gap between that position and practice — a minority of organisations apply human-grade controls to agents. **The execution boundary:** DoorDash's Flux is a named organisation moving agent execution off developer laptops and onto a platform it controls and audits, and the published reason is the one a director recognises — a laptop caps resources, depends on a device staying connected, and can hand an autonomous agent live credentials. Zalando is the counterweight case: two and a half years in, a shared proxy and a deliberate refusal to mandate a tool, with the control expressed as a risk-graded release gate rather than a tool policy. **Policy at runtime:** Microsoft's governance architecture is the cleanest published statement that policy becomes governance only when production can produce the proof, expressed as a four-function loop over nine domains. **Build or buy:** Salesforce's Dreamforce announcements are the in-window turn — the control surface an engineering leader is being asked to build internally is now a shipped product with a name, which converts a platform roadmap item into a make-or-buy and supplier-concentration decision.

Zalando = agent-mode with a human gate; the auto-approval applies to the *change*, not to the agent. Microsoft = architecture, band-agnostic by design. ### S4 — Re-gating the lifecycle: spec, build, verify, release
**Layout: standalone with a prominent inline SVG (Figma's precision climb against its own enable threshold).**
Walks the stages. **Spec** — spec-driven development turns the specification into the maintained artefact and, with constitution and checklist features aimed at compliance, into the audit trail; that changes who owns it and who signs it off. Covered **qualitatively, with no numbers** (§7 bars the adoption and success-rate claims; Issue 23's restriction carries). **Build** — Böckeler's harness memos name the artefact the platform or enablement function now owns and maintains: the guides and sensors that constrain what an agent may do, separated into feedforward mechanisms and feedback mechanisms, with harness templates predicted to become shared organisational assets the way service templates did. Thoughtworks' Radar v34 is the analyst-grade version of the same claim and supplies the section's named risk, **cognitive debt** — code volume outrunning the humans who have to hold the system in their heads — plus the observation that the inflection is about technique rather than technology. GitHub's enterprise-managed permissions are the in-window-adjacent proof that the build-stage decision right is now centrally administrable: what an agent may do to a shell, to files, and to network domains is set by an administrator and cannot be weakened downstream. **Verify** — Figma published the cost of making a review gate trustworthy: hand-labelling eight weeks of false positives to lift precision from 15% to 80%, an adjudicator pass for recall, and a precision bar set before the agent was allowed to address developers at all. **Release** — LinearB's benchmark is the largest independent dataset in the pool on where the lifecycle now queues: AI-authored pull requests wait far longer to be picked up and are accepted at a fraction of the manual rate.

Figma = agent-mode review agents with a human gate; agent-generated pull requests default to draft. **Reconciliation subheading is mandatory.** The argument: these are not contradictory readings, they are readings taken at different points of the same redesign. The left column is what a handful of organisations that finished the work can publish; the right is what the population looks like part-way through it. The reconciliation is that a control surface is not a control — a registry, a proxy, a sandbox and a review agent produce governance only once someone owns them, the lifecycle stages around them have been re-gated, and the evidence they emit is the evidence somebody actually asks for. Partial redesign returns partial value, which is exactly Forrester's finding restated at the organisational level rather than the team level.

### Lessons & implications
**Layout: `.lessons-section` navy, opening `.verdict-box`, closing `.key-takeaway`.**
**The verdict (one italic paragraph, the issue's thesis):** the control surfaces are arriving faster than the ownership. Every limb of an agent operating model can now be bought — identity, execution boundary, policy runtime, review gate, spend meter — and buying them is the easy half. The organisations whose numbers hold up are the ones that answered the ownership questions first: who approves an agent, who sets the bar a gate must clear before it speaks to a developer, who holds the token budget, and who signs off a change nobody wrote. Where those answers exist, the evidence a regulated customer asks for falls out of the lifecycle as a by-product. Where they do not, the control plane is a purchase order.
**Key takeaways (`.key-takeaway`, teal arrow bullets, shorthand stat references only — never re-explain):**
- Oversight is at least four distinct activities, not one review step — a capacity plan that budgets "review" is short before it starts.
- Identity is the enforcement point: a policy that has not been expressed as an agent identity with an owner and an expiry is a document.
- The AI bill is variable, generated by engineering choices, and currently belongs to nobody in particular — the ownership decision precedes the budget decision.
- The precision bar a review gate must clear before it addresses developers is a leadership decision, not a tuning parameter.
- Regulated customers will ask for evidence the lifecycle produced, not assurance written about it — and most of those expectations pre-date agents.
- A bought control plane is a supplier-concentration decision as well as a governance one.
- Redesigning one stage and leaving the rest returns a fraction of what the stage-level numbers promise.
