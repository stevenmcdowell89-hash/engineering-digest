Foreword

This was a week about what an organisation actually owns. Uber redrew its own shape — how many layers it will carry, how small a team it will run. Nvidia agreed to buy a platform eighteen million developers depend on and none of them own. A football club stopped renting a hypervisor licence and found the exit took three months. Each is a boundary redrawn in public with a number attached, which is rarer than it sounds.

Quote of the week

"The difference between a medicine and a poison is dosage."

— Martin Fowler, martinfowler.com, 2 September 2026

Fowler's point is that few programming practices are absolute goods or evils, and that the useful questions are about context and dose. Two practitioners argued independently this week that fewer changes should reach a human reviewer — an argument about dosage, applied to a practice most organisations run at a fixed rate. martinfowler.com →

Management & teams

Uber publishes the levers behind a 10% reduction (2 min)

Concept of the week Span of control

The number of people reporting directly to one manager. It is a design choice rather than an accident of hiring, and it decides how many management layers an organisation needs for the same headcount.

Widen it and managers come out of the structure; narrow it and layers go in.

Uber told staff on 2 September that it is cutting about 10% of its team — roughly 3,300 roles on TechCrunch's reporting, its largest reduction since the pandemic. What sets the announcement apart sits underneath the number: Uber published figures against four levers of org design, and named the org chart as the object being redesigned.

Four levers, with numbers attached

Three of the four are about shape. Uber says it has "reduced the number of employees who sit 7+ layers from the CEO by 20%" — layers being the reporting distance between an engineer and the chief executive — and cut micro-teams, those of one or two reports, by nearly 50%. The fourth is location: about 1% of employees will be remote, and the rest are on three days a week in the office.

What Uber redesignedWhat Uber says changed
Overall headcountReduced by about 10%
Employees seven or more layers from the CEOReduced by 20%

The structural half

Core Services Engineering and Science are being combined into one organisation, mirroring the structure Uber runs on Mobility and Delivery, and the three Delivery Ops teams across Restaurants, Retail and Direct become single-threaded teams — one team with one owner for an end-to-end area. Dara Khosrowshahi frames the result as "a simpler org chart geared toward building versus managing", and presents the reduction as structural rather than a judgement on individual performance — his framing, and the one anyone reading the memo will be asked to interpret.

Issue 22 covered Oracle reducing headcount while its capital spending nearly tripled; Uber offers org design as the whole of its case.

What changes

The levers a large engineering organisation actually pulled are now public and numbered, which changes what a director can be asked to justify. Span of control is now a figure a peer has published, and the conversation moves towards the smallest team an organisation will run and the layers it will defend. The location policy in the same memo is the part that needs explaining, because org shape and where people sit get read as one message about trust.

Sources: Uber, TechCrunch, HR Executive (2 September 2026)

What Nvidia has agreed to pay for Hugging Face

Nvidia agrees to buy the registry the open-model world runs on

$12,930,300,000 is NVIDIA's own figure for the acquisition it confirmed on 3 September. Reporting puts the structure at about $11.9bn to shareholders plus up to $1bn in equity-based retention awards — share grants that vest over time for employees who join — and the close in the first half of 2027. NVIDIA's own post carries neither.

Hugging Face is a model registry: the shared place teams pull models and datasets from, and publish to. NVIDIA's figures put more than 3 million models, 500,000 datasets and 1 million applications there, used by over 18 million developers, researchers and creators and more than 200,000 companies. NVIDIA is already the platform's largest contributor of open models and data.

The commitment attached is that "Hugging Face will remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem", with developers keeping their choice of models, frameworks, clouds, inference providers and computing platforms, and no requirement to run NVIDIA compute. Open weights — model files anyone can download and run — stay downloadable on that account. It is also the platform OpenAI reported its agents reached in pre-release testing.

The takeaway: a dependency almost every engineering organisation carries in its model supply chain has changed owner, and the platform's openness now rests on a commitment from its new owner.

A football club does the Broadcom exit in public

"Savings is a big part of the reason. It's a significant amount, north of 85 percent," Tottenham Hotspur's CTO Rob Pickering told The Register on 3 September, describing what the club stopped paying after moving off VMware to HPE GreenLake over about three months. The 85% is his figure and is not audited.

>85%
Licensing saving on Pickering's figure — migration in about three months

The replacement hypervisor — the layer running many virtual machines on one physical server — is HPE's VM Essentials, which Pickering calls a "completely capable replacement". He puts more weight on the consolidation than the money: the estate connected back into one operations stack his team can work from, "one pane of glass", is worth more to him than a vendor-to-vendor saving. He cites the commercial changes since Broadcom's takeover of VMware as part of the reason for going; Broadcom, in the same window, said it will curate and secure open-source artefacts for its Spring, Java, Python and Node.js customers.

The takeaway: the Broadcom exit many infrastructure leaders are modelling privately has now been done in public, on a three-month timescale, by a named organisation putting a number on it.

Feature

Nobody noticed (2 min)

Jargon Watch Autonomous agents

The word covers a spectrum. At one end sit agent features inside developer tools, where a person triggers each step and reads the result.

At the other sit agents with authority to act against live systems, with no per-step approval. The agents here are at that end, and they are the attacker's.

The intrusion started at a public API endpoint — an interface one system exposes for others to call — and finished, less than ten hours later, with an eighty-page report on the victim's own security weaknesses — written by the software that had just exploited them. Unit 42, Palo Alto Networks' threat-intelligence team, published it on 2 September. AI agents ran every stage, at the far end of the spectrum: operated by an attacker against a live estate, nobody approving each step.

Ten hours, end to end

Reconnaissance, the breach of the endpoint, mapping of internal services, scraping repositories for hard-coded credentials, then the secret-management system holding those credentials centrally and the master administrative access inside it. From there the agents validated access across cloud, identity, container and hosted-software environments and hijacked the build-and-release pipeline to take cloud keys. The attacker had an agent leave behind "an 80-page, technical audit detailing dozens of exploited findings". Unit 42 puts the run at "less than 10 hours" against "around two weeks" for human operators, and names neither the victim nor any researcher.

The $600,000 nobody billed

METR — a research nonprofit that measures whether and when AI systems might threaten catastrophic harm — published its own account on 31 August. An attacker stole an API key in March 2026 and consumed roughly $600,000 of model credits over three weeks unnoticed. The key sat on a researcher's personal cloud instance, publicly accessible behind an authentication layer containing "a fail-open vulnerability that silently disabled authentication" — fail-open being where a control that cannot reach its check waves the request through.

METR gives three reasons the usage went unseen. It was accustomed to running evaluations that use large volumes of tokens, so heavy consumption looked ordinary. Its internal usage dashboard did not show rate-limited requests. And because it was not paying for those tokens, there was no natural spend ceiling. It has since expanded monitoring and added spend alerts to keys where possible.

What the two have in common

Both organisations' detection assumptions were calibrated to conditions that no longer held. One assumed an intrusion takes long enough to notice. The other assumed abuse eventually turns up on an invoice. The failure in each case was not a missing control but an unwritten one, and unwritten assumptions appear on no register. Issue 23's deep dive found the constraint moving from writing code to verifying it; both incidents are about verifying what something already did.

The takeaway: detection in both organisations rested on assumptions about time and cost that no longer hold, and assumptions of that kind sit outside every control register that gets audited.

Sources: Unit 42 (2 September), METR (31 August)

In Practice Making technical decisions when not the SME

Handed a team whose work he had never done (2 min)

JT
Jarryd Trokis Engineering Manager, Monzo Monzo · April 2022 · a classic worth revisiting
Read the full piece →

"I was entrusted with the care of the Backend Platform Squad, a team whose skills are far beyond my domain of expertise." Jarryd Trokis was a frontend engineer — not the subject-matter expert for anything the team did — when Monzo gave him that squad — a small team owning one area — running the infrastructure everything else at the bank sits on. Kubernetes, the system that schedules and runs services across a fleet of machines, was one of the things he had never operated, and so was most of what his engineers did daily.

His account of the first three months reads as a journey, and the gap stays open throughout. What he describes finding is that he could still take part in conversations he could not have led, wading in to offer help or insight until, in his phrase, the problems came looking for him.

"As a humble frontend engineer … I could not have felt more out of place and out of my depth."

— Jarryd Trokis, Monzo

He is careful about what he credits for the team's results. He points at the environment the team works in — autonomy, support and fast feedback — and at the people in it. That is Trokis describing Monzo; the reading that follows is this publication's.

From a director's chair, an account like that changes what is worth looking at when a manager is placed over unfamiliar ground. The useful question becomes whether the conditions the team works in are ones the manager is actually holding: whether feedback arrives quickly enough to act on, whether the team has room to decide, whether support is real when something breaks. None of that needs technical judgement about the work itself.

What to try Trokis's account suggests a manager newly placed over unfamiliar ground can be assessed on the conditions they hold for the team, and those conditions are visible inside a quarter.

The Leadership Read

Code review is the wrong place to catch most of it (3 min)

Rachel Laycock's argument is that mandating human review of every change bundles several jobs into one late gate — knowledge sharing, teaching, ownership, architectural alignment, catching defects — and that the gate is now a bottleneck, because more code is produced than can realistically be read. Her subject is code-generating assistance inside developer workflows, where an engineer triggers the work and reads the result — a different thing from an agent acting on its own against a live estate.

RL
Rachel Laycock CTO, Thoughtworks martinfowler.com · September 2026
Read it →

The arithmetic of the queue

She puts the shape of the problem in one sentence: "If an agent can produce ten times the code but every line eventually queues up waiting for a senior engineer to inspect it, we haven't created a ten-times engineering organisation, we've created a big backlog and a new bottleneck." The gate was affordable while the volume reaching it was set by how fast people could type.

Moving the judgment earlier

Her first move carries her own label, "Shift the judgment left": pairing, mob programming — mobbing, where the whole team works on one problem together — and collaborative design sessions held before implementation rather than after. The deterministic checks — formatting, linting, security scanning, the ones with a single correct answer — are automated. Architectural constraints are encoded as what she calls fitness functions: automated tests asserting a property of the system as a whole rather than of one change.

What stays at the gate

Her second label is "Review by exception". Human review is reserved for architectural decisions, security boundaries and unfamiliar critical systems; the rest is trusted to the checks and practices that ran earlier. Most review is performed on a diff — the line-by-line difference between the old and new versions of a file — and her objection to that unit lands in one line.

"We need engineers to understand systems, not diffs."

— Rachel Laycock, martinfowler.com

Why it matters now

The claim underneath both labels is that review is several activities wearing one name, and the ones genuinely needing a human are a minority of those currently mandated. Pairing, mobbing and collaborative design carry the knowledge sharing and teaching; automation takes the deterministic checks; what remains at the gate is judgement about structure and risk, a much smaller queue than most organisations run. Shevchenko argued for a version of the same change the same week, in a talk reported separately.

Read the full article: martinfowler.com →

This could be worth talking about: of the things code review currently does — teaching, ownership, architectural alignment, defect-catching — which would still get done if review were reserved for architectural decisions, security boundaries and unfamiliar critical systems?
