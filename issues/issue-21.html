Foreword

On 2 August the European Union's transparency rules for AI systems became enforceable, and the work they create belongs to engineering. The rest of the week ran the same way. Control kept moving out of instruction and into structure: a token budget with a dashboard behind it, a wallet with a hard ceiling.

Wider engineering landscape

The EU's AI transparency rules are live, and the work sits in the pipeline (3 min)

Article 50 of the EU AI Act became enforceable on 2 August. It is the transparency article: people must be told when they are dealing with an AI system, and machine-generated content must be marked so that a machine can identify it as such. The obligations are short to read and awkward to implement, because the marking has to happen where the content is produced.

Jargon Watch Provider vs deployer

A provider places the AI system on the EU market — builds it, or puts its own name on it. A deployer uses one under its own authority.

Article 50 splits the obligations between them. Disclosure of the AI interaction and machine-readable marking of generated output sit with the provider. Telling people they are exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation, and disclosing deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest content, sit with the deployer.

Most organisations are both, on different systems. Article 50 text →

What the article requires

Providers of AI systems that interact directly with people must disclose that the interaction is with an AI, unless that is obvious from the context. Providers of systems generating synthetic content — audio, image, video or text generated or manipulated by an AI system — must mark the output in a machine-readable format. That means a watermark or embedded metadata that lets a machine determine the content was AI-generated. Deployers carry a different set: informing people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation, and disclosing deepfakes and AI-generated content on matters of public interest, unless it went through substantive human editorial review.

WhoWhat Article 50 now requires
Provider — places the system on the EU market Disclose that a person is interacting with an AI, unless obvious (50(1)); mark generated audio, image, video or text machine-readable and detectable (50(2)). Live 2 August 2026; systems already on the market have until 2 December 2026 for marking.
Deployer — uses it under its own authority Inform people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation (50(3)); disclose deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest content, unless under substantive human editorial review (50(4)). Live 2 August 2026, no backstop.

Where the obligation bites

Scope follows the EU market. The obligation attaches to placing an AI system on the EU market, or to the system's output being used within the EU. Incorporation elsewhere does not remove it, and a UK or Middle East firm sits outside the article only where neither its system nor its output reaches people in the EU. The penalty ceiling sits in Article 99: administrative fines of up to €15m or 3% of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher. For SMEs and start-ups, Article 99(6) makes it whichever is lower.

Two speeds on the same clock

Content generated and published before 2 August needs no retroactive labelling. Generative systems already on the market have until 2 December 2026 to meet the machine-readable marking obligation — a transitional window granted by the Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted by Parliament on 16 June and Council on 29 June 2026, and not present in the original Act. Systems placed on the market from 2 August got no such window. The same instrument pushed the separate high-risk regime under Annex III out to 2 December 2027.

Where the work lands

Article 50 is not a disclosure policy to be written; it is a marking capability to be built. Machine-readable marking is work in the generation pipeline and the model-serving layer, and detectability has to survive the content leaving the system that made it. The AI Office's Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content — voluntary companion guidance carrying standardised labelling icons, already signed by several major providers — grants signatories a presumption of conformity, which is the closest thing to a published specification anyone has. Organisations that filed the article with legal two years ago are discovering it now has a delivery date.

Microsoft puts a number on every engineer's token spend

“Tokenmaxxing is not what we are optimizing for,” Microsoft EVP Jay Parikh wrote in a note to the company's Core AI organisation, surfaced by reporting on 4 August. Tokenmaxxing is an internal coinage: chasing token throughput as though volume were the goal. Alongside the instruction came division-level AI token budget targets, an internal dashboard letting employees and their managers track individual token consumption in real time, and a standardisation of internal work on the cheaper GPT-5.6.

The memo is internal and unpublished. Every figure and quote here is as reported and none of it is sourced to Microsoft directly; reporting puts individual engineers' spend in the hundreds to a few thousand dollars a month.

The dashboard is the part that changes a leadership conversation. A per-person consumption meter, visible to managers, arrives in the same week as an instruction not to optimise for the thing it measures. What that instrument is permitted to be used for — capacity planning, chargeback, or performance signal — is a decision an organisation makes once and then lives with.

One combined leadership job, split into two peer roles

The product-and-technology job comes apart at both ends of the scale

On 29 July, Temenos announced a leadership change that took effect on 3 August. Barb Morgan steps down as Chief Product and Technology Officer — CPTO, one person owning both functions. Cormac Flanagan becomes Chief Product Officer and Tony Coleman returns as Chief Technology Officer, and both now report to the CEO as peers, where both roles previously reported into the CPTO. Sriraman Ganesan continues as global head of product engineering. Temenos sells core banking software: the system of record running accounts, balances and payments, on top of which a bank's other software is built.

Temenos product and technology leadership, before and after 3 August 2026.

Six days later the same structural move appeared at a very different scale. Demis Hassabis moves from DeepMind CEO to a new Chair role plus Alphabet Chief Scientist, with CTO Koray Kavukcuoglu becoming an SVP who takes day-to-day operations and reports to Sundar Pichai. Semafor reports that transition had been in train for roughly a year. Separately, on the same day, Jeff Dean left after 27 years, alongside Sanjay Ghemawat, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le, to found Discovery Loop, with Google as founding investor and cloud partner.

Two organisations, orders of magnitude apart, reached the same conclusion within a week: setting direction and running the operation are different jobs.

The takeaway: the combined product-and-technology role is being unbundled at both ends of the market, which puts the reporting line — peers under the CEO, or nested under a single owner — back on the table as a live design question.

In Practice Handling reorgs and ambiguous mandates
A classic worth revisiting

Twenty-five reorgs in thirteen years

BB
Ben Balter Director of Hubber Enablement, GitHub June 2026
Read the full piece →
Concept of the week Scope renegotiation

Here, scope means the remit of a role — what a person or team is accountable for — and not the boundary of a project.

Renegotiating it means agreeing with a new manager what stays, what moves and what gets handed on. A reorg opens that conversation briefly, because nobody's remit is settled yet. Once the new chart hardens, the same request reads as someone trying to drop work.

25
reorgs
13
managers
13
years

Twenty-five reorgs. Thirteen managers. Thirteen years. Ben Balter counted them up at GitHub: a new org chart roughly every six months, and a new boss roughly every year. The gaps between restructures ran from six weeks to fifteen months, so the six-month average sits between two very different experiences.

In Balter's account a reorg is not a crisis; it is the baseline condition of working in a large engineering org. What he describes is an environment in which the org chart is the least stable object in it. Reporting lines dissolve mid-project. A manager who held the history moves to a different part of the tree. Decisions taken in a room with six people become unfindable once four of them have moved on. Across twenty-five of these, the things that survived were consistent enough to name.

  1. Relationships stay portable. The people who vouch for a piece of work are not tied to the reporting line the work was done under.
  2. Decisions live at a linkable URL. Documented durably rather than held in the head of whoever happened to be in the room.
  3. Expectations are reset deliberately. With a new manager, inside the first two weeks, before assumptions harden into a story about what someone does.
  4. Scope gets renegotiated while the window is open. The reorg is the moment to shed legacy work, and the window closes quickly.
You don't get angry at rain—you buy a raincoat.

The lesson holds without clicking through: what survives a reorg is reputation, relationships, and work that was shipped, documented and linkable. Anything that existed only inside a reporting line leaves with the reporting line.

What to try Every one of Balter's four mechanisms has to be in place before an announcement lands, which makes them a standing property of how an organisation works and a reasonable thing for a leadership team to look at in a quiet quarter.

Klarna's support metrics counted deflection and speed (3 min)

Klarna told investors in November 2025 that its AI customer-service agent was doing the work of 853 full-time agents and had saved $60m. In the same quarter, its customer service and operations costs rose to $50m, from $42m a year earlier. What the rollout claimed

In February 2024 Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month — two-thirds of all customer service chats, across 23 markets and more than 35 languages. It put the volume at the work of 700 full-time agents. Resolution time fell from 11 minutes to under two. Customer satisfaction, or CSAT, was reported on par with human agents, and repeat inquiries fell 25%. Klarna projected a $40m profit improvement for 2024.

What happened next

By May 2025 the chief executive was revising the account. Sebastian Siemiatkowski, speaking to Bloomberg: “As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality.” He added that “really investing in the quality of the human support is the way of the future for us.” Klarna reopened hiring for human support. Over the same period total company headcount fell about 40%, from roughly 5,000 to roughly 3,000 — the product of a hiring freeze and attrition, which is the detail most retellings of this story drop. In September 2025 the company redeployed staff into customer service from marketing, engineering, legal, operations and analytics; those people had first been told their roles were “no longer a priority” and placed in a talent pool.

Every metric Klarna published measured deflection and speed: share of chats handled, full-time-agent equivalents, minutes to resolution. None of them measured whether the resolution was any good. The chief executive's own account is that cost became the predominant evaluation factor and quality fell out of the bottom. What a rollout measures is what it optimises, and quality was not in the published metric set.

The line back to the Lead

Article 50 makes telling a person they are talking to an AI a build requirement. Klarna is the case study in what happens when nobody measures whether the AI was any good.

The takeaway: an AI support rollout that publishes only deflection and speed metrics has already decided what it optimises for, and the correction arrives eighteen months later at the cost of rehiring.
