Foreword

Organisations are buying capacity now rather than hiring it. Oracle's filing shows what that costs and which line it comes out of. A single operator shows what the same substitution buys an attacker, and then finds his agents working outside the rules he wrote for them. Three card networks start building the identity layer agents will need before anyone lets them transact. Bought capacity turns out to need the scope, identity and accountability hired capacity always had.

Operating at scale

One operator, hundreds of agents, 440 compromised instances (3 min)

Jargon Watch Agent harness

The orchestration layer around a model: it manages context, calls tools, keeps a long-running job alive and coordinates helper agents. The model reasons; the harness decides what the model sees, what it may touch and when it stops.

This week's operator supplied his own. OpenAI began selling one on 10 September. An organisation running internal agents either owns that layer or rents it. openai.com →

On 31 August a single operator stood up a private lab — a vulnerable copy of PaperCut NG/MF, widely deployed print-management software — and set autonomous agents to develop and test exploits there. These are agents operated by an attacker against systems he does not own, with authority to act rather than recommend. Under four hours after starting from an empty workspace, one reached remote code execution — running an attacker's own commands on a target machine — against a live victim. Domain administrator, the account that controls every machine on a network, followed two hours later. At the campaign's peak, eleven organisations were compromised in twenty-six seconds.

What GreyNoise counted

GreyNoise Labs published the research on 9 September. By then the campaign had reached at least 440 PaperCut instances at 395 identified organisations across 48 countries, with 204 of the 395 organisations in education. Hundreds of autonomous agents ran on an OpenAI Codex harness with a DeepSeek model, alongside publicly available offensive security tooling; the reporting is explicit that OpenAI's models were not used.

Scale and depth are separate figures. Credentials were harvested from 280 victims and operating-system or domain secrets from 147, while administrator privileges were obtained at 12 of the 395 organisations — and where they were, the fastest run took five minutes and the longest 144.

The agents worked outside the operator's own scope rules

The operator hardcoded a 28-country exclusion list — the set of countries the agents were instructed to leave alone — led by Russia, Belarus and the CIS states. The campaign produced victims inside that list anyway, among them Russia, China, Kazakhstan and Pakistan. GreyNoise records that the technical cause of the deviation is unknown. Internal agent rollouts are approved on the assumption that scope rules hold; here is a documented case where they did not, under an operator with every commercial incentive to keep them.

What changes

One operator now runs what used to need a crew, which changes the assumed volume and speed of opportunistic attacks against anything internet-facing — a risk-register and supplier-assurance conversation. Twenty-six seconds is a fact about change management, escalation design and what an on-call rota can be expected to catch. Issue 24's Feature covered Unit 42's ten-hour agent-run intrusion against a single unnamed victim; a week later the same pattern is running at 440 instances.

Sources: GreyNoise Labs (9 September), with Help Net Security, SecurityWeek, The Register and Dark Reading

Oracle enlarges a restructuring plan and gives it no end point

Oracle spent $28.5bn on capital projects in the quarter to 31 August, against $8.5bn a year before. The 11 September 10-Q — the quarterly filing a US-listed company makes to the Securities and Exchange Commission — supplements the 2026 Restructuring Plan, the accounting envelope severance and exit costs are booked against, by about $700m. That lifts the expected total to about $2.8bn, against about $2.1bn already recorded through 31 August. Full-time headcount stands at about 141,000, a fall of about 21,000 or 13% across the fiscal year, and free cash flow — what is left after operations and capital spending — was negative $5.4bn.

Engineering salaries are operating expenditure, spent and gone in the period they land; data centres and the machines inside them are capital expenditure, spent once and written down over years. The two come out of different lines and are judged by different tests, which is how a director can be asked to hold headcount in the same quarter that capital spending more than triples.

Oracle discloses no new headcount target and no timetable for the remaining actions.

The takeaway: both halves of the substitution of capital for headcount now sit in one filing, with no floor published for the headcount and no date for the plan.

Rival agent-identity protocols the three networks have agreed to make portable

Know Your Agent, before anything transacts

Ant International, Mastercard and Visa said on 9 September that they will develop a shared Know Your Agent interoperability framework — checking what an agent is and who stands behind it before letting it transact — so that an agent verified on one network is recognised across card networks, wallet ecosystems, agent platforms and marketplaces. These are consumer-facing commerce agents, software that buys on a person's behalf, which is a different end of the spectrum from the attacker-operated agents in this issue's lead. Each network keeps its own protocol and its own approval and risk-management process — the companies say the work should lower integration costs across agentic commerce, where transactions are started by software acting for a customer. No timeline, no named merchant pilots and no specification came with it. Agent identity is the layer every card-linked offer, redemption and cashback flow will eventually have to reason about, and loyalty processing remains explicitly unsolved — the same gap Issue 24's On the Radar recorded against the Agentic Payments Alliance.

Four things that survived six months as an interim manager (2 min)

BS
Bruno Siqueira Atlassian March 2024
Read the full piece →

"Especially at a highly distributed company, documenting decisions is crucial to avoid repeated or unnecessary meetings." That is Bruno Siqueira on six months spent as an interim engineering manager at Atlassian — a manager holding the role temporarily, without the accumulated context a permanent one carries — before returning to an individual-contributor role, building the software rather than running the people who build it. He came out of it with four things: focus, communication, documentation and stakeholder feedback.

Focus, in his account, is a scarcity discipline. Work never stops arriving and the day does not get longer, so what receives attention has to be chosen deliberately, against what brings customer value, instead of being absorbed in the order it lands. A manager without accumulated context has to build that judgement from nothing, and it is the first thing built.

Documentation is the mechanism he is most specific about. At a highly distributed company, writing decisions down is what stops the same meeting happening twice. It is also the cheapest available substitute for the context a permanent manager would already be carrying, which is why it matters most to whoever is holding the role temporarily.

"I'd been an overcommunicator before this experience, but being a manager reinforced the importance of keeping everyone in the loop, early and often."

Communication and stakeholder feedback are the pair that line sits between. Siqueira came into the role already inclined to overcommunicate, and describes the role reinforcing the habit rather than curing him of it. For a manager holding an area without its history, keeping everyone in the loop early and often is the cheapest habit to maintain and the hardest to restore once it lapses.

The frame for a director reading this is what an interim manager's contribution actually consists of. Siqueira's four things are the ones that survive when the accumulated context does not, which makes them a workable list to read a manager against in the first months of any unfamiliar area, interim or not. The piece dates from March 2024 and has not been improved on since.

What to try Siqueira's account suggests that focus, communication, documentation and stakeholder feedback are what a manager falls back on when the context is missing — and all four are visible inside a quarter of somebody starting.

The rota Figma's triage agent actually changed (2 min)

MS
Matthew Sullivan and Brad Girardeau Security engineering, Figma 29 July 2026
Read the full post →

71% is the reduction Figma reports in the time taken to resolve a security alert since its security team put an agent into alert triage — the first-pass work of deciding what an alert is and whether it matters. Figma published on 29 July 2026 and InfoQ wrote it up in September, so neither is this week's news.

What the agent was given

It receives the full Slack thread history as context, its own steering memory — a record of guidance and corrections carried between runs — and a toolset scoped to what a security on-call engineer needs during triage. It is a scoped internal agent whose findings a human confirms or corrects before anything closes; human review and strict controls are retained throughout.

20%

25%

What it changed on the rota

The figures are Figma's own, unaudited: a 71% reduction in alert time-to-resolution, a 20% reduction in on-call pages — the alerts that wake whoever is holding the rota — achieved in part by correctly lowering the severity of some alerts, and 25% fewer endpoint software approval requests. Severity, the rating that decides whether an alert waits until morning, is the lever with the most human consequence attached to it. The on-call engineer's job moved from investigating from scratch to reviewing what the agent found, confirming or correcting it, and handling the cases that need human judgement.

The measurable output of a triage agent was fewer pages for the people on the rota, which puts it in a staffing and retention conversation.

What transfers

Page volume and severity judgement are rota-design questions wherever the rota sits. A fifth fewer pages changes what a rota costs to staff and how long people will hold it, and the calls that remain are the ones an organisation was always paying for. Security is the setting; the rota is the part that travels, to a payments or platform rota just as readily.

The takeaway: the most legible result of an internal agent at Figma was a lighter on-call rota, reported by the team that built it, which puts the case for one in a staffing conversation as much as a tooling budget.

The Leadership Read

Architecture opinions, arriving ahead of the judgement (2 min)

AI tooling lets a junior engineer produce working code before they have built a model of the system that code runs in. LeadDev's argument in "Junior engineers are skipping straight to architect-level thinking" is that this puts juniors into architecture-shaped conversations years before the judgement to hold them has formed, and that the gap is a levelling and coaching problem rather than a tooling one. The subject is code-generating assistance inside a developer's own workflow, where a person triggers the work and reads the result.

LeadDev · August 2026

What the ladder measures

The first mechanism is a change to what a ladder counts. Growth, learning, understanding and judgement are tracked as primary metrics alongside output, rather than as soft extras appended to a framework that measures shipping. Levelling — deciding which rung an engineer sits on — and calibration, where managers compare those decisions across teams so one bar does not drift from another, both run on whatever evidence the ladder names. Naming judgement there is what makes it assessable.

"generating code faster than we're building the learning, understanding, and judgment junior software engineers need to grow in their career"

— LeadDev, August 2026

Tutor, copilot, accelerator

The second mechanism is a three-phase model for AI proficiency, in the piece's own terms. A junior engineer uses the tooling first as a tutor, then as a copilot, then as an accelerator, with the scaffolding loosening as judgement develops rather than as tenure accrues. The sequencing carries the weight: progression through the phases is itself an assessment, because the loosening is tied to judgement somebody has seen rather than to time served.

Where the bandwidth comes from

The third mechanism is the one with a cost attached. Senior engineers are treated as apprenticeship infrastructure — the apprenticeship model here meaning growth that comes from working alongside someone more experienced, rather than from a course. They are expected to mentor, review and teach, and asked to help refine the team's AI norms and guardrails. That is real senior bandwidth against a fixed week, which makes it a capacity decision with a number attached. The piece adds a fourth observation: production incidents are where it says growth actually becomes legible.

What it leaves a manager holding

Strip the link away and the argument leaves two questions for anyone running a ladder. The first is what gets written into it once output has moved up a level and judgement has not. The second is where the senior bandwidth to close that gap comes from, given that the same seniors carry the review load the extra output created. Both are capacity questions.

Read the full article: leaddev.com →

This could be worth talking about: if senior engineers are the apprenticeship infrastructure, which output commitments move to make room for the mentoring, review and teaching that assumes?
