Operating at Scale · Active Threat

The agentic attacker arrives for real (4 min)

Over a single weekend in early July, something inside Hugging Face's infrastructure was working without rest. It read datasets, escalated its own privileges, moved between systems, and logged action after action — more than seventeen thousand of them — before anyone noticed. The operator was software: an autonomous production agent with action authority, a model wired to tools end-to-end, calling them without per-step human approval and pursuing a goal an attacker had set.

Jargon Watch Agentic attacker / autonomous production agent

An autonomous production agent has action authority — it calls tools end-to-end (reading data, moving between systems, running commands) with no human approving each step.

That is a different thing from the "agent mode" inside a developer tool, where a person still triggers the task and reviews the result. An agentic attacker points that unsupervised autonomy at a target.

What Hugging Face disclosed

In a disclosure published on 16 July, Hugging Face described an end-to-end AI-agent intrusion — and said its own defensive models detected and reconstructed the sequence. The chain runs in four moves:

  1. A poisoned dataset entered the pipeline — a supply-chain entry point, the agent-era version of prompt injection (hidden instructions smuggled in through data an agent reads and trusts).
  2. The agent used that foothold to escalate its own privileges.
  3. Across the weekend it carried out more than 17,000 recorded actions, operating faster and longer than a human operator would.
  4. Hugging Face's defensive AI flagged the behavioural pattern and reconstructed the full timeline after the fact.

The same week, twice more

It did not land alone. Around 10 July, researchers documented JadePuffer — described as the first ransomware campaign run end-to-end by an AI agent, entering through a known CVE in Langflow (an open-source builder for LLM-powered apps). Days later, on 14 July, Ant Group open-sourced SingGuard-NSFA, an agent-guardrail framework built to intercept exactly this class of behaviour — the defender's response, arriving inside the same news cycle as the attacks.

The asymmetry worth noting

The uncomfortable detail for engineering leaders is the imbalance. The attacker runs an unconstrained model. Defenders reaching for commercial AI mid-incident can be throttled or refused by their vendor's own safety filters at the worst moment. That is the case for defensive-model readiness — capable models on owned infrastructure before an incident, not scrambled for during one. The live conversation is about supply-chain trust in datasets and dependencies, and whether an organisation could reconstruct an agent's actions the way Hugging Face did.

Forward-deployed engineering becomes an industry model

Build-versus-buy gained a third option this quarter: embed. On 30 June, AWS announced a $1B Forward Deployed Engineering practice — forward-deployed engineering (FDE) being vendor engineers embedded inside a customer to build alongside them, then leave the team self-sufficient. On 2 July, Microsoft followed with a $2.5B "Frontier Company", committing to place some 6,000 engineers inside customer organisations for agentic-build work.

The mechanics are concrete. AWS describes pods of five to six engineers, roughly 45-day cycles, and fixed-outcome pricing — the vendor is paid for a result, not for hours. Named early customers include LSEG and Unilever. The framing that matters for engineering leaders is a third path alongside building in-house and buying a finished product: renting a team that builds the intelligence with the customer, then hands it back.

Circle wins an OCC national-trust-bank charter

On 10 July, Circle received final approval from the OCC to establish First National Digital Currency Bank, N.A. — an OCC national trust bank, meaning a federally supervised trust charter rather than a state one. That places custody of USDC (Circle's dollar-pegged stablecoin) under national oversight. Circle's own release avoids a dollar figure and calls USDC "the world's largest regulated stablecoin"; secondary coverage puts circulation around $73B.

Chief executive Jeremy Allaire framed the approval as "a defining step in bringing blockchain technology and digital assets into the core of the U.S. financial system." For teams working in the loyalty and commerce-media patch, the signal is that the settlement layer beneath their world is professionalising under bank-grade supervision.

The takeaway: stablecoin custody is moving inside the regulated banking perimeter, which makes USDC a more predictable building block for anyone designing payments or rewards flows on top of it.

A recent read

From SSH to REST: retiring direct access to production data clusters

MV
Mahendran Vasagam Staff Software Engineer, Slack Slack Engineering · June 2026
Read the full piece
"When you SSH into a machine and run a command, you're creating a direct, stateful connection."

That sentence is where Slack's data-platform team started, and it names the problem precisely. SSH — a direct remote shell into a machine — was how jobs reached Slack's production EMR clusters (Amazon's managed big-data cluster service). It worked, but it meant standing SSH keys, direct human access to production, and orchestration tightly wound around each cluster's internals.

The fix was Quarry — Slack's REST job-submission gateway that sits between services (chiefly Airflow, the workflow scheduler that orchestrates their data jobs) and the compute engines that run the work. Instead of opening a shell, a service now makes an authenticated REST call. Short-lived service-to-service tokens replace long-lived SSH keys, and direct human SSH into production EMR goes away entirely — the security driver behind the whole effort.

DimensionBefore — SSHAfter — REST / Quarry
Execution modelDirect shell, stateful connectionStateless authenticated REST call
Security surfaceStanding SSH keys, human access to prodShort-lived service-to-service tokens
State trackingHeld in the live connectionTracked by the gateway
Airflow–EMR couplingTight, bound to cluster internalsDecoupled behind the API

The migration moved 700+ jobs across 7 operator types over three quarters — spanning 5 teams and 8 data regions — with no downtime. The security gain came with a second benefit. By decoupling Airflow from EMR's internals, the team also unblocked future moves such as Spark-on-Kubernetes, because the orchestration layer no longer knows or cares what runs the job.

What to try A security-driven modernisation reads as a compliance chore until it is framed as a multi-quarter bet that buys future optionality — here, a clean API boundary that made the next migration possible rather than harder.

Concept of the Week Span of control

The number of direct reports a single manager holds. Meta's push to 50:1 stretched managers so thin that coaching, calibration and feedback thinned with it — which is why the walk-back to about 20:1 followed the morale backlash.

A typical engineering span sits around 6–8:1. The wider the ratio, the more a manager's job shifts from developing people to processing them.

Buy infrastructure, build intelligence (5 min)

At Owner.com, a system that routed sales leads to the right rep worked well — until the engineer who built it moved on. Its accuracy quietly decayed. Reps noticed leads landing in the wrong hands and drifted back to assigning them by hand. Nobody decided to abandon the tool. It simply stopped being trusted, and the organisation's understanding of how it worked left with its author.

The framework

Brendan J Short, writing in The Signal and drawing on Owner.com's chief revenue officer Kyle Norton, sets out a rule for the AI era: buy your infrastructure, build your intelligence. Buy the reliability layer — dialers, CRM plumbing, enrichment, call recording, security. That work is undifferentiated and someone else runs it better. Own the intelligence: the definition of an ideal customer, the scoring and prioritisation logic, the judgement that actually distinguishes one company's approach from another's.

What to keep hold of

The distinction lands on a handful of control dimensions. Retain control of the data. Keep an understanding of the decision logic rather than accepting an opaque output. Preserve the ability to export organisational memory, the capacity to evaluate the quality of what the system produces, and the portability to migrate that accumulated learning to another vendor. Lose those and the productivity is real but rented — and the learning compounds on someone else's balance sheet.

"The worst version is a black box that owns both the workflow and the memory. You get productivity, but the learning compounds somewhere else."

— Kyle Norton, CRO, Owner.com

Two cautionary examples

Short offers two. In the first, a "swimlanes" tool over-consumed API calls, broke compliance rules, and disrupted the workflows it was meant to smooth — the lesson being that a bought tool doing core work still needs dedicated engineers and roadmap discipline behind it. In the second, Owner.com's lead-routing system worked at launch and then quietly decayed once its builder moved on. A system that is nobody's job to maintain degrades until people route around it; launch is where the maintenance work begins.

Why it matters this week

This is the non-binary frame for the decision the hyperscalers are now forcing. AWS and Microsoft have made "embed our engineers to build it with you" a priced product this month. Short's rule is the discipline that keeps that option honest: let a partner build the reliability layer and even the first version of the intelligence, but keep the data, the logic, and the memory in-house, where they compound for the organisation over time. The vendor hands the team back and moves on; the accumulated judgement stays behind.
