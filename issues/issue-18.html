Foreword

In the first week of July, consumer-authorised AI agents completed their first real, settled purchases at live European merchants — agentic commerce, meaning commerce where an AI agent, acting for a person within limits they set, carries the checkout through to completion. The distinction matters: these are consumer-authorised, agent-mode checkouts operating within parameters the buyer sets, not autonomous production agents with standing authority to move money on their own. In the same seven days, a 140-firm consortium moved to rewrite the dollar-settlement layer those purchases will run on.

Two announcements, one phenomenon. The checkout flow and the money underneath it are converging into a single commerce stack, and the card-linked-offer and rewards vertical that many banks’ partners operate in sits directly in its path. Read together, they earn a full issue.

Two developments in one week describe one shift. On 2 July, at the Visa Payments Forum in Paris, Visa reported the first live agentic-commerce transactions in Europe: consumer-authorised agents browsed, chose and paid at real merchants across more than 30 European issuers. On 30 June, a consortium of over 140 firms — including Visa, Mastercard and Stripe — unveiled Open USD, a stablecoin engineered to hand nearly all of its reserve income back to partners rather than a single issuer.

The rails are live but small; the money is assembling but not yet moving. One half is a working pilot at four merchants; the other is a launch partner list with no settlement volume until later in 2026. Read together, they sketch a new commerce stack in which the buyer is no longer assumed to be human and the settlement layer is no longer a fixed, single-issuer utility. This issue works through both halves in turn — the rails in Source 1, the money in Source 2 — then asks, in the Counterpoint, whether this is a stack genuinely forming or a convergence arriving ahead of its demand.

Source 1 — The Rails

The rails go live

A checkout no human clicked through

At the Visa Payments Forum in Paris on 2 July, Visa reported something it had only ever described before. In the transactions it showed, an AI agent browsed products, selected items and initiated purchases at live European merchants — among them lastminute.com, Frasers and BrickDepot — while a real cardholder sat behind the whole thing, having set the terms in advance. Visa described the agents as “acting within consumer-defined parameters” and the transactions as “explicitly authorised by the consumer.” The consumer sets the limits; the agent acts inside them; the transaction settles on Visa’s network with one of more than 30 European issuers in the loop.

The point worth holding onto is where on the spectrum this sits. Industry language lumps everything from a tool running a multi-step task under human supervision together with fully autonomous production agents that hold their own credentials and spending authority. These are firmly at the supervised end: a person authorises the purchase and defines the envelope, and no standing spend authority is handed to software.

The consumer authorises; the agent acts within set limits; nobody hands standing spend authority to a bot. The deep-teal node is the new layer — the part that verifies an agent to a merchant.

The layer that is actually new

What makes the merchant trust an agent it has never seen is the piece that did not exist eighteen months ago. Visa’s Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP) lets a merchant recognise and trust a verified AI agent, and the Agent Directory is the registry those agents are listed in; both sit under Visa Intelligent Commerce, the umbrella programme enabling agent-initiated payments on Visa’s network. Crucially, TAP is designed to complement rather than replace OpenAI’s Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP) — an open specification for agent-run checkout — with TAP answering “is this agent who it claims to be?” and ACP handling whether the payment instrument works for the merchant and amount.

This is where the week reads as a crossing rather than a claim. Mastercard opened the protocol race with Agent Pay back in April, which the digest covered in Issue 15; through the spring it was announcements and sandboxes. The 2 July event moved one leg of that race from specification to a settled transaction at a named merchant.

The protocol race, and the moment it produced a real settled purchase.

For now the footprint is deliberately narrow — a handful of merchants, a defined set of issuers, tightly scoped consumer controls. The agents are live. The open question is what they will settle over.

The money gets rewritten

Zero-fee

The bigger structural move happened two days earlier and involved no agents at all. On 30 June, an entity called Open Standard launched Open USD (OUSD), a stablecoin — a digital token pegged one-to-one to a currency, here the US dollar, and backed by reserves held against it — assembled behind a launch roster of more than 140 firms: Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, BlackRock, BNY, Standard Chartered, Coinbase and others spanning payments, banking and crypto.

The economics are the story

Two design choices set it apart. Businesses can mint and redeem OUSD — create new tokens against dollars paid in, and destroy them to pay dollars back out — at no cost and with no volume caps. And the reserve revenue-sharing is inverted: the interest earned on the dollars backing the token, minus a small management fee, flows back to the partner ecosystem rather than to one issuer. Circle keeps the reserve income on USDC; OUSD is structured to return nearly all of it to participants, under an independent partner board. Stripe went further than membership, positioning OUSD as the base stablecoin for its commerce ecosystem. Circle’s 17.55% drop on the day underlined the stakes, as analysts recast the contest from token-versus-token to ecosystem-versus-ecosystem.

Incumbent shares are illustrative; the load-bearing, verified figures are 140+ partners, zero volume today, and Circle’s single-day fall.

Why this belongs in the same issue as Source 1

OUSD is the settlement substrate that agentic-commerce flows are built to plug into. An agent-initiated checkout has to settle over something, and whoever owns that layer sets the economics for everyone downstream — including the merchants, partners and loyalty programmes that sit at the end of the chain. When the settlement rail shares reserve revenue with its partners instead of retaining it, the merchant and partner model shifts with it.

Near the settlement fork sits x402 — an emerging open standard for machine-to-machine payments over the web, reviving the long-dormant HTTP 402 “Payment Required” response so agents can pay programmatically.

OUSD is not live. It is expected to launch later in 2026, starting on Solana, with a broader rollout to Polygon, Stellar and Aptos to follow. Today it settles nothing. What it has instead is a launch roster large enough to make the settlement layer a contest rather than a given.

Counterpoint

Real, or premature?

The names on both announcements are as large as payments gets. The volume behind them is close to nothing. That gap is the whole question — a working pilot at four merchants on one side, a launch roster with zero settlement volume on the other — and it is worth holding the momentum and the reality side by side before drawing the line.

Why the contradiction resolves

The apparent contradiction — the biggest names in payments behind something with negligible volume — resolves because platform standards are chosen before they scale. The architecture is being set now precisely while the volume lags; that is how settlement layers and protocols have always been adopted. For an engineering organisation in the commerce-media and loyalty patch, the signal is directional rather than immediate: the sensible response is to map the change and prototype against it, not to bet a roadmap on this quarter’s pilot numbers.

Case Study

Visa’s live European pilot, step by step

Stripped to its path, the transaction Visa reported on 2 July is a short chain of five moves — and the one that did not exist before is the verification step in the middle.

One agentic transaction, start to finish

Step 1 — Authorise. The consumer sets the conditions for a purchase — what to buy, within what limits — and authorises an agent to act inside that envelope.

Step 2 — Browse & select. The agent, in agent-mode, browses products and selects items at a live merchant — lastminute.com, Frasers or BrickDepot.

Step 3 — Verify. The Trusted Agent Protocol verifies the agent to the merchant, which recognises it as a trusted, registered agent rather than a bot to block.

Step 4 — Settle. The purchase settles over the Visa network, with the consumer’s authorisation and controls carried through the transaction.

Step 5 — Issuer in the loop. One of more than 30 European issuers stands behind the payment, exactly as it would for a card transaction a human clicked through.

What separates this from the announcement stage

Everything before this week lived in decks and sandboxes. The reason 2 July counts is that each of those five steps happened against real merchants with real issuers behind them. The footprint is small and the controls are tight, but the path is complete — which is what makes it a reference point rather than a preview.

Lessons & Implications

Two assumptions just weakened

The Verdict

For a fintech in the loyalty and commerce-media patch serving banks, two long-held assumptions weakened in one week: that the buyer is human, and that the settlement rail is a fixed, single-issuer utility. Card-linked-offer attribution, rewards logic and verification and fraud posture now have to account for an agent-initiated buyer and a programmable, revenue-shared settlement option.

1. A non-human buyer changes attribution and verification

When the entity at the checkout is an agent acting for a person, the questions that underpin card-linked offers and rewards — who bought, on whose behalf, through which channel — need new answers. Agent verification (the TAP-style question of whether an agent is trustworthy) becomes a first-class part of the fraud and attribution surface.

2. The settlement layer is now a strategic choice

OUSD makes the rail a decision rather than a default. The incumbent path (USDC, USDT) and the partner-revenue-share path (OUSD) carry different economics for everyone downstream. That is a choice engineering and commercial leaders will increasingly have to hold an opinion on.

3. Interoperability is the thing to watch

The durable signal this week was structural: TAP is being built to align with ACP. Where the protocols interoperate is where the standard settles — a better predictor than any one vendor’s launch.

Key Takeaway
  • Map where a non-human buyer changes card-linked-offer and rewards attribution, and where agent verification enters the fraud surface.
  • Treat the settlement layer as a strategic choice: incumbent USDC/USDT versus partner-revenue-share OUSD.
  • Watch protocol interoperability (TAP aligning with ACP) above any single vendor’s announcement.
  • It is early — track and prototype against the change; do not re-platform on pilot-stage evidence.
